# Wendy Lite: ESP32 Microcontrollers Join the Wendy Platform

Wendy is a Physical AI platform for robots, drones, and edge devices. The goal: one simple tool and one consistent workflow for all your hardware. Build, test, simulate, and ship your application with the same commands, over USB, across your local network, or through the cloud.

But Physical AI is rarely just about a robot and the compute board driving it. Linux-class boards like NVIDIA Jetson or Raspberry Pi may do the heavy lifting, but a real-world deployment is usually a complete system: one or more robots, a local or cloud server, and a whole network of distributed sensors feeding them context. Temperature probes, presence detectors, cameras, microphones: these are the eyes and ears that let a physical system understand its environment.

Most of these sensors run on microcontrollers, and few microcontroller platforms are as capable, affordable, or widely adopted as Espressif’s ESP32. That’s why we’re introducing Wendy Lite: support for ESP32 microcontrollers inside the Wendy platform. Same tool, same experience, from the robot down to the smallest sensor.

## How It Works

First, flash Wendy Lite onto your board. Connect it over USB and run:

```bash
wendy install
```

Select your board, enter your Wi-Fi network and password, and that's it. Your device now runs Wendy Lite, joins your Wi-Fi network, and is reachable on your local network or remotely through Wendy Cloud.

## Build and Run an App

Let's build and run a classic blink program:

```bash
wendy init blink-demo --template blink-rgb-led
cd blink-demo
```

This creates a regular ESP-IDF project:

```text
blink-demo/
├── CMakeLists.txt
├── README.md
├── sdkconfig.defaults
├── wendy.json
└── main/
    ├── CMakeLists.txt
    ├── idf_component.yml
    ├── main.c
    ├── rgb_led.c
    └── rgb_led.h
```

The additional `wendy.json` file makes that project a Wendy Lite app, which can be deployed with just one command:

```bash
wendy run
```

`wendy run` builds your project with ESP-IDF. The only prerequisite is the ESP-IDF Installation Manager (EIM). The Wendy CLI handles the rest: it connects to the device, flashes the app, runs it, and streams its logs back to your terminal. This works over USB and Wi-Fi, and remotely through Wendy Cloud.

## Full ESP-IDF Support

Your app is a regular ESP-IDF project, so you have full access to the ESP-IDF ecosystem and full control over your device.

What makes it a Wendy Lite app is the `wendy_core` component. It manages connectivity and lets you update the app over a local connection or through the cloud. Include and initialize it first in `app_main()`:

```c
#include "wendy_core.h"

void app_main(void)
{
    ESP_ERROR_CHECK(wendy_core_init());
    // your code here
}
```

Already have an ESP-IDF project? Run `wendy run` in its folder. The CLI offers to create `wendy.json` and to add the `wendy_core` component if they are missing. Your `sdkconfig` stays in your hands, and a few of its settings need to match what `wendy_core` expects. We encourage you to start from one of our templates or tutorials, whose `sdkconfig.defaults` already has the right settings.

## Wendy Cloud

You can use Wendy Lite entirely on your local network, with no cloud account. But when your sensors are spread across a building or a field, enroll them in Wendy Cloud and reach them from anywhere. Log in once, then `wendy install` can enroll the board while flashing it, or you can enroll it later over USB:

```bash
wendy auth login
wendy device enroll
```

Enrollment gives each device its own identity, built on proven security standards: keys generated on the device that never leave it, X.509 certificates with SPIFFE identities, mutual TLS 1.3, and certificate chains signed with post-quantum ML-DSA (FIPS 204). Once enrolled, only your organization can reach the device, whether over the local network, Bluetooth, or the cloud.

## Supported Chips

Wendy Lite runs on these ESP32 targets:

- ESP32-C5
- ESP32-C6
- ESP32-C61
- ESP32-S3
- ESP32-P4
